\section{Divide and Conquer: Capped Map Search}
\label{sec:dc}

\subsection{Problem}
\label{sec:dc-problem}

Airbnb's map search returns the listings inside the visible map window, but
never more than a fixed number of them. When a window holds more, the rest
are cut off and cannot be reached from that window. A market study of a city needs \emph{every} listing, so a scraper
must choose map windows small enough that none is truncated, using as few
searches as possible. This problem came up while scraping Miami, FL and
Rinc\'on, PR~\cite{danial2023airbnb}; a single city-wide window returned only a
small fraction of the listings.

\subsection{Abstraction}
\label{sec:dc-abstraction}

Reported coordinates have fixed precision (Airbnb gives at most five
decimals), so we measure locations in grid units and model the map as
$\mathbb{Z}^2$. Let $P$ be a set of $n$ \emph{listings}, each with a location
in $\mathbb{Z}^2$. Several listings may share a location; let $m$ be the
largest number sharing one. A \emph{window} is a half-open rectangle
$r=[x_0,x_1)\times[y_0,y_1)$ with integer corners; its \emph{side} is
$\max(x_1-x_0,\,y_1-y_0)$, and $P_r$ denotes the listings located in $r$. The
search region $R$ is a window with $P_R=P$ and side $W$.

The search service is an oracle with cap $k\ge1$:
\begin{equation}
Q_k(r)=\begin{cases}
P_r & \text{if } |P_r|\le k,\\
\textsc{overflow} & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:oracle}
\end{equation}
The problem is to compute $P$ from $R$ and $Q_k$ using as few oracle calls
(web requests) as possible.

Splitting a window at $x_m=\lfloor(x_0+x_1)/2\rfloor$ and
$y_m=\lfloor(y_0+y_1)/2\rfloor$ gives four \emph{quadrants}
$r_1,\dots,r_4$. Splitting recursively yields a
\emph{quadtree}~\cite{finkel1974quad,samet1984quadtree} whose leaves are the
windows actually read.

\subsection{Algorithm}
\label{sec:dc-algorithm}

\textsc{Collect} (Fig.~\ref{fig:dc-alg}) queries a window. If it is not
truncated, its listings are the answer. Otherwise the window is divided into
four quadrants, each is solved recursively, and the answers are united. The
full answer is $\textsc{Collect}(R)$.

\begin{figure}[t]
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE \textbf{function} \textsc{Collect}$(r)$
\STATE $A\gets Q_k(r)$
\IF{$A\neq\textsc{overflow}$}
  \RETURN $A$ \COMMENT{window fits under the cap}
\ENDIF
\STATE $r_1,\dots,r_4\gets$ quadrants of $r$ \COMMENT{divide}
\RETURN $\bigcup_{i=1}^{4}\textsc{Collect}(r_i)$ \COMMENT{conquer, combine}
\end{algorithmic}
\caption{Enumerating the listings in window $r$ through a capped oracle $Q_k$.}
\label{fig:dc-alg}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Correctness}
\label{sec:dc-correctness}

We first show that any answer \textsc{Collect} produces is right
(Theorem~\ref{thm:correct}), then that it always produces one when $m\le k$
(Theorem~\ref{thm:depth}).

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:partition}
Every location in $r$ lies in exactly one quadrant of $r$, and no location
outside $r$ lies in any quadrant.
\end{lemma}
\begin{IEEEproof}
Since $x_0\le x_1$ we have $x_0\le x_m\le x_1$, and likewise for $y_m$. For
$(x,y)\in r$, exactly one of $x<x_m$ and $x\ge x_m$ holds, and exactly one of
$y<y_m$ and $y\ge y_m$; the pair selects exactly one quadrant. A location
outside $r$ violates one of the four bounds of $r$, and every quadrant keeps
or tightens each of them.
\end{IEEEproof}

Because the windows are half-open, a listing on a split line falls in exactly
one quadrant, so it is neither lost nor counted twice.

\begin{theorem}[partial correctness]\label{thm:correct}
If $\textsc{Collect}(r)$ terminates, it returns $P_r$, every leaf window holds
at most $k$ listings, and every location in $r$ lies in exactly one leaf
window (and no location outside $r$ in any).
\end{theorem}
\begin{IEEEproof}
Strong induction on the height $h$ of the recursion tree. If $h=0$, the oracle
did not overflow, so $|P_r|\le k$ and line~4 returns $Q_k(r)=P_r$; the only
leaf is $r$ itself. If $h>0$, the algorithm recurses on the quadrants
$r_1,\dots,r_4$, whose recursion trees are shorter than $h$, so by induction
$\textsc{Collect}(r_i)=P_{r_i}$, the leaves under $r_i$ hold at most $k$
listings, and each location is in exactly $[p\in r_i]$ of them. By
Lemma~\ref{lem:partition} each listing of $P_r$ lies in exactly one $r_i$, so
$\bigcup_i P_{r_i}=P_r$; and the number of leaves containing a location $p$ is
$\sum_i[p\in r_i]=[p\in r]$, again by Lemma~\ref{lem:partition}.
\end{IEEEproof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:unit}
A window of side at most $1$ contains at most one location. Hence if
$m\le k$, such a window holds at most $k$ listings and is never split.
\end{lemma}
\begin{IEEEproof}
If $x_0\le x<x_1\le x_0+1$ then $x=x_0$, and likewise $y=y_0$, so
$P_r$ consists of listings at $(x_0,y_0)$, of which there are at most $m\le k$.
\end{IEEEproof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:halving}
If $r$ has side at most $2B$, each quadrant of $r$ has side at most $B$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{IEEEproof}
With $S=x_1-x_0\le 2B$, the two horizontal halves have widths
$x_m-x_0=\lfloor S/2\rfloor$ and $x_1-x_m=\lceil S/2\rceil$, both at most $B$;
the same holds vertically.
\end{IEEEproof}

\begin{theorem}[termination and depth]\label{thm:depth}
If $m\le k$ and $r$ has side at most $2^D$, then $\textsc{Collect}(r)$
terminates with recursion depth at most $D$. In particular
$\textsc{Collect}(R)$ has height at most $\lceil\log_2 W\rceil$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{IEEEproof}
Induction on $D$. If $D=0$, the side is at most $1$, so by
Lemma~\ref{lem:unit} the oracle answers and the recursion stops at depth $0$.
For $D+1$: if the oracle answers, we are done; otherwise the quadrants have
side at most $2^D$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:halving}, so by induction each recursive
call terminates within depth $D$, and $\textsc{Collect}(r)$ within depth
$D+1$.
\end{IEEEproof}

\begin{corollary}[total correctness]\label{cor:total}
If $m\le k$, $\textsc{Collect}(R)$ terminates within depth
$\lceil\log_2 W\rceil$ and returns $P$; its leaf windows partition $R$ and
each holds at most $k$ listings.
\end{corollary}
\begin{IEEEproof}
Theorem~\ref{thm:depth} gives termination, Theorem~\ref{thm:correct} the rest.
\end{IEEEproof}

The condition $m\le k$ is also necessary. If more than $k$ listings share a
location, every window containing it overflows, and no strategy using $Q_k$
can reveal them.

Every statement in this subsection is machine-checked in Lean~4~\cite{lean4}
(Appendix~\ref{app:dc-lean}), under the following correspondence:
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
Lemma~\ref{lem:partition} & \texttt{quadrants\_partition}\\
Theorem~\ref{thm:correct} & \texttt{collect\_correct}\\
Lemma~\ref{lem:unit} & \texttt{unit\_window\_fits}\\
Lemma~\ref{lem:halving} & \texttt{quadrants\_side}\\
Theorem~\ref{thm:depth} & \texttt{collect\_terminates}\\
Corollary~\ref{cor:total} & \texttt{collect\_total}
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Lean accepts a recursive
definition only with a termination argument, so its \textsc{Collect} takes
the remaining depth budget as an explicit parameter and returns $\bot$ if the
budget runs out. Theorem~\ref{thm:depth} says that the budget
$\lceil\log_2 W\rceil$ never runs out. The closest existing formalizations,
region quadtrees in Isabelle~\cite{nipkow2024quad,nipkow2024afp} and $k$-d
trees in Isabelle and Coq~\cite{rau2019kdtree,hamid2023kdtree}, obtain
termination from a fixed grid exponent or from the point count shrinking at
each median split. Neither covers a tree that splits space over arbitrary
points.

\subsection{Running Time}
\label{sec:dc-analysis}

Let $N$ be the number of oracle calls and $T$ the total work when a node with
$|P_r|$ listings spends $\Theta(|P_r|+1)$ time returning or distributing them.
Let $h$ be the tree height.

Windows at one depth are disjoint (Lemma~\ref{lem:partition}), so each level
holds at most $n$ listings and at most $\lfloor n/(k+1)\rfloor$ split windows,
each holding at least $k+1$ listings. Internal nodes sit at depths
$0,\dots,h-1$ and have four children each, so
\begin{equation}
\lceil n/k\rceil\le N\le 1+4h\Big\lfloor\frac{n}{k+1}\Big\rfloor,
\qquad T=O\big(n(h+1)\big).
\label{eq:general}
\end{equation}
The lower bound holds because the leaves cover $P$ with at most $k$ listings
each. By Theorem~\ref{thm:depth}, $h\le\lceil\log_2 W\rceil$, so
$T=O(n\log W)$: the cost is governed by the coordinate resolution, not by $n$
alone.

In the balanced case, where each split sends about $n/4$ listings to each
quadrant (true in expectation for uniformly random locations),
\begin{equation}
T(n)=4\,T(n/4)+\Theta(n)\quad(n>k),
\label{eq:recurrence}
\end{equation}
with $T(n)=\Theta(1)$ for $n\le k$. With $a=b=4$ and
$f(n)=\Theta(n)=\Theta(n^{\log_b a})$, case~2 of the Master
Theorem~\cite{cormen2022introduction} gives $T(n)=\Theta(n\log n)$. Unrolling
the recursion shows the dependence on $k$: the tree is complete with
$L=\lceil\log_4(n/k)\rceil$ levels below the root, each costing $\Theta(n)$, so
\begin{align}
T(n)&=\Theta\!\left(n\log\frac{n}{k}\right),\label{eq:balanced}\\
N&=\sum_{i=0}^{L}4^i=\frac{4^{L+1}-1}{3}=\Theta\!\left(\frac{n}{k}\right),
\label{eq:requests}
\end{align}
within a constant factor of the $n/k$ requests any strategy must make.

\subsection{In the Language of the Problem}
\label{sec:dc-domain}

Search the whole city in one window. If the results fit on the pages the site
shows, record them. Otherwise cut the window into four equal tiles and repeat
on each. A listing exactly on a cut goes to the tile right of or above it, so
it is recorded once. Dense neighborhoods are zoomed into repeatedly, while
open water is dismissed after one search. By \eqref{eq:requests}, the number
of searches is proportional to $n/k$, the number of listings divided by the
number shown per search, and the zoom never goes deeper than
$\lceil\log_2 W\rceil$ levels. With five-decimal coordinates over a one-degree
span, $W=10^5$ and $17$ levels suffice. The method fails only at an address
with more listings than the cap, such as an apartment building whose units
share rounded coordinates. No zoom level separates them, which is why
Corollary~\ref{cor:total} needs $m\le k$. In Rinc\'on at most $7$ listings share a location, so any $k\ge7$
works.

\textbf{Setup.} \textsc{Collect} was implemented in Python~3.14
(Appendix~\ref{app:dc-code}) and run on an AMD Ryzen~7 9800X3D. The oracle is
simulated. Each node partitions the listings it holds among its quadrants,
which is the $\Theta(|P_r|)$ step of \eqref{eq:recurrence}. Coordinates are
double-precision numbers in the unit square, i.e.\ the grid model at
resolution $2^{-52}$; the implementation also stops at windows narrower than
$10^{-9}$ and flags them, which never happened. We used $k=50$ and
$n=2^{10},\dots,2^{19}$ listings, either uniform or drawn from $20$ Gaussian
clusters ($\sigma=0.02$). Each size was run five times on fresh random inputs;
we report the fastest run with garbage collection disabled, as
\texttt{timeit} does~\cite{python-timeit}. Every run was checked to return
each listing exactly once.

\textbf{Running time.} Fig.~\ref{fig:dc-scaling}(a) shows measured time
against the fitted model. Fig.~\ref{fig:dc-scaling}(b) divides time by
$n\log_2(n/k)$. Over a $512\times$ range of $n$ the ratio stays between
$26$ and $43$\,ns (uniform) and between $36$ and $48$\,ns (clustered), confirming
$\Theta(n\log(n/k))$. The zig-zag arises because the tree gains a level only
when $n$ grows about fourfold, so the true cost rises in steps. The slight rise
at the largest sizes is consistent with cache effects. Clustered inputs cost
about $20\%$ more because their trees are two levels taller.

\textbf{Requests.} Table~\ref{tab:dc-requests} compares requests on uniform
inputs with the exact balanced count \eqref{eq:requests}; they agree within
$3.1\%$. Fig.~\ref{fig:dc-scaling}(c) shows the count growing linearly,
parallel to the lower bound $n/k$.

\textbf{Crowding.} To test the depth bound, we fixed $n=10{,}000$ uniform
listings and added $k+1=51$ listings inside a square of side
$\delta=2^{-2},\dots,2^{-28}$. Isolating that square requires windows of side
about $\delta$, i.e.\ a grid of $W=2^e$ units when $\delta=2^{-e}$.
Fig.~\ref{fig:dc-depth} shows the tree height tracking
$\log_2(1/\delta)=\log_2 W$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:depth}) although $n$ is fixed,
and each extra level adding about four requests, the $4h$ term of
\eqref{eq:general}.

\textbf{Real data.} Fig.~\ref{fig:dc-rincon} runs the algorithm with $k=20$ on
$261$ Airbnb listings from Rinc\'on, PR (scraped March 29, 2023~\cite{danial2023airbnb}).
It used $57$ requests, $43$ windows and height $5$, within the bounds
$14\le N\le 241$ of \eqref{eq:general}. The two dense clusters are split
repeatedly; the outskirts are covered by a few large windows.
